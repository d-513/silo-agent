# Hubs beat swarms; batch the wake-ups

Research from 2025–2026 says multi-agent LLM systems gain from three things: more total compute, a clean context for each subtask, and wall-clock parallelism on breadth-first work. Agents talking to each other is not one of them. xAI's Grok 4 Heavy is officially just "parallel test-time compute", meaning independent attempts compared at the end. Grok 4.20 Multi-Agent is a leader that synthesizes the work of 4 or 16 server-side sub-agents, and xAI does not document how those sub-agents communicate. The strongest controlled study (Kim et al., GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 families, 260 configurations) finds the average multi-agent change against a single agent is about **−0.3%**. The sign depends on the task: **+81%** on decomposable financial analysis, **−39% to −70%** on sequential planning, and diminishing or negative returns once a single agent already scores above about **45%**. Orchestrator-led systems amplify errors **4.4×** where peer-messaging systems amplify them **7.8×**. In production, every team found that tried flat peers moved to a hierarchy. Messaging between agents mid-run ships only as an experimental Claude Code feature, and nobody has published a measured gain for it. On timing, the industry has moved from blocking delegation to a lead that ends its turn and is woken on completion. One 2026 benchmark, whose existence this report could not cross-check, finds that frontier leads do slightly worse when results arrive one at a time than when they arrive together. For Silo, this supports its hub-and-spoke shape, depth of 1, no peer channel, end-turn-and-wake, and lead-written taskboard. The evidence argues for narrowing what gets delegated, batching wake-ups per spawn round, and adding an independent-reviewer pattern. The main gap: no study on frontier models isolates the value of mid-run messaging between agents.

## Grok's multi-agent modes are parallel sampling with a judge

The production systems sold as "multi-agent" fall into three families, and the families have different evidence behind them. **Grok 4 Heavy** (2025-07-09) is officially described only as "parallel test-time compute" that lets it "consider multiple hypotheses at once". It was the first model to reach **50.7%** on the text-only subset of Humanity's Last Exam, and it scored **61.9%** on USAMO'25 ([xAI](https://x.ai/news/grok-4)). Musk's description is that agents "do work independently, and then they compare their work. Often only one of the agents actually figures out the trick" ([Built In](https://builtin.com/artificial-intelligence/grok-4)), and that the result is "not always majority vote" ([AiNews](https://www.ainews.com/p/xai-launches-grok-4-and-grok-4-heavy-aims-to-redefine-ai-intelligence)). That makes it a relative of best-of-N with a learned or prompted comparator, not delegation. Claims of "16 parallel instances" come from SEO pages only.

**Grok 4.20 Multi-Agent** (consumer beta 2026-02-17; API `grok-4.20-multi-agent`, March 2026) is a different product. Several agents are launched "to discuss and collaborate", and a leader "is responsible for synthesizing the discussion and presenting the final answer". `reasoning.effort` low or medium gives **4 agents** and high or xhigh gives **16**. Every agent's tokens and tool calls are billed. Client-side function calling and custom tools are **not supported**, and sub-agent state comes back only encrypted ([xAI docs](https://docs.x.ai/developers/model-capabilities/text/multi-agent)). xAI does not say whether the agents exchange messages round by round or are separate rollouts that the leader reads. The named roles (Harper, Benjamin, Lucas) and the hallucination-reduction figures of 65% to 83% come from X posts and aggregators, which disagree with one another ([tokenmix](https://tokenmix.ai/blog/grok-4-20-multi-agent-review-2026)). Treat them as marketing. The one documented design choice that matters is that xAI ties its effort setting to **agent count**, not thinking depth, which treats parallel agents as the main way to spend more compute.

| Family | Systems | Measured gain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same task, parallel samples, then aggregate | Grok 4 Heavy; GPT-5 Pro and GPT-5.5 Pro ([system card](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03267v1)); Gemini Deep Think ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/advanced-version-of-gemini-with-deep-think-officially-achieves-gold-medal-standard-at-the-international-mathematical-olympiad)); Cursor 2.0's 8 parallel agents with a human picking ([changelog](https://cursor.com/changelog/2-0)) | HLE 50.7%; IMO gold, 35/42 |
| Hub-and-spoke delegation, no peer messages | Anthropic Research ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)); Kimi K2.5 Agent Swarm ([arXiv 2602.02276](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02276v1)); Cursor planners and workers ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)); OpenAI Agents SDK handoffs and agents-as-tools ([OpenAI](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/multi_agent/)) | +90.2% on an internal eval; BrowseComp 60.6 → 78.4 |
| Agents messaging peers mid-run | Claude Code agent teams ([docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)); Grok 4.20 Multi-Agent (channel undocumented) | None published |

**Kimi K2.5** stands out because its delegation is **trained**. An orchestrator learned through reinforcement learning spawns frozen sub-agents with independent memory, and only task-relevant outputs come back. The reward explicitly penalizes "serial collapse" (never parallelizing) and "spurious parallelism" (spawning without real decomposition). Cost is counted in critical steps: the orchestrator's steps plus the *longest* branch. That yields BrowseComp 60.6 → 78.4, WideSearch item-F1 72.7 → 79.0, and a **3–4.5×** wall-clock reduction ([arXiv 2602.02276](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02276v1)). (A second note gives the WideSearch baseline as 72.8, a minor discrepancy.) The paper reports no token cost for swarm runs. Gemini Deep Think combines its parallel branches inside one model's reasoning, so it is not agents with tools ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2025/08/gemini-2-5-deep-think)).

## Delegation wins on breadth and loses on sequential work

The best controlled evidence comes from Kim et al., "Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems" (Google Research, MIT et al.). It compares one agent against four multi-agent topologies on GPT-5-nano through GPT-5, Gemini 2.0 Flash through 2.5 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 3.7 through 4.5. Prompts, tools and total reasoning tokens are held equal ([arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3)). The journal version reports **260 configurations** over six benchmarks, SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench among them ([Nature Machine Intelligence, via MIT](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/capable-language-models-can-outgrow-the-benefits-of-collaboration/)). The headline gains and losses come from the same experiment, so task structure decides the sign:

| Task | Best multi-agent result | Worst multi-agent result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finance-Agent (decomposable analysis) | Centralized +80.8% | Hybrid +73.1% |
| BrowseComp-Plus (wide search) | Decentralized +9.2% | Independent −35% |
| WorkBench (tool-heavy) | Decentralized +5.6% | Centralized −1.2% |
| SWE-bench Verified | Hybrid −2.1% | Independent −14.9% |
| Terminal-Bench | Independent +1.7% | Centralized −19.2% |
| PlanCraft (sequential planning) | Hybrid −39.1% | Independent −70.0% |

Two results from the study generalize. First, **capability saturation**: once a single agent scores above roughly 45%, coordination gives diminishing or negative returns (β̂ = −0.404, p < 0.001). On SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench, the threshold predicted the effect of coordination in **94%** of validation configurations ([MIT](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/capable-language-models-can-outgrow-the-benefits-of-collaboration/)). Second, **efficiency**: a single agent produced **67.7 successes per 1,000 tokens**, against 42.4 for independent agents and 13.6 for the hybrid topology, which carried **+515%** token overhead and averaged 44.3 turns ([arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3)).

Studies that equalize thinking tokens point the same way. Tran and Kiela (Stanford, April 2026) find that a single agent matches or beats multi-agent setups on multi-hop reasoning under equal thinking budgets. Multi-agent wins only when the single agent's context is badly corrupted or when more total compute is spent. They also warn that API budget artifacts, especially with Gemini 2.5, inflate apparent multi-agent gains ([arXiv 2604.02460](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460)). Their models are Qwen3, a DeepSeek-R1 distill and Gemini 2.5, with budgets of about 10K tokens, so the result says little about long-horizon agent work.

The practitioner record agrees once the compute is accounted for. Anthropic's Opus 4 lead with Sonnet 4 subagents beat a single Opus 4 by **90.2%** on an internal research eval. It used about **15× the tokens of chat**, and token usage alone explained **80%** of the variance on BrowseComp ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). The eval is unpublished and has no token-matched single-agent control. Anthropic also notes that "upgrading to Claude Sonnet 4 is a larger performance gain than doubling the token budget on Claude Sonnet 3.7", and it rules out "most coding tasks" because they have few truly parallel parts. Cognition's April 2026 follow-up reaches a similar rule: things work when "multiple agents contribute intelligence to a task while writes stay single-threaded". Parallel writers still fail because their implicit choices about style and edge cases conflict. The most reliable second agent is a reviewer that shares **no prior context** with the coder: Devin Review catches about **2 bugs per PR, 58% of them severe** ([Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)). A "smart friend" escalation to a stronger model works only "when both models are strong".

Net: delegation pays for breadth-first, read-heavy, decomposable work. It also pays as **context isolation**, where a subagent spends tens of thousands of tokens and returns a 1,000–2,000-token summary ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). LangChain measured 67% fewer tokens processed in one multi-domain test ([LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/choosing-the-right-multi-agent-architecture)). It loses on sequential, tightly coupled or tool-heavy work, and on accuracy for tasks a strong model already does well alone.

## Wake-on-completion beats blocking, but results land better together

Production moved decisively away from blocking. In June 2025, Anthropic's Research lead waited on each batch of subagents, and Anthropic named the costs: the lead "can't steer subagents", and a slow subagent stalls it. Anthropic put off async execution because of "result coordination, state consistency, and error propagation" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). By late 2026, a Claude Code subagent spawned with nothing configured "starts in the background". Its results "reach Claude as a completion notification in a later turn", marked as an automated event and not a user message, and waiting is now the opt-in ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents)). Cursor took the same path. "We tried giving agents a tool to explicitly wait on another agent's work, but they rarely used it." Workers now send one handoff (what was done plus "notes, concerns, deviations, findings"), which the planner receives as a follow-up message ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)). Earlier, Cursor had named "planners should wake up when their tasks complete" as the next step ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Codex, by contrast, gathers summaries when its threads complete and lets the user steer a running subagent ([OpenAI Codex docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/subagents)).

Research on making the orchestrator itself non-blocking shows latency gains, but only with training. Microsoft's AsyncThink lets a model fork workers and keep reasoning until it joins them. On Qwen3-4B it matched parallel thinking's accuracy at **28% lower** critical-path latency. The format without reinforcement learning scored near zero, and without a concurrency reward the model collapsed toward sequential behaviour ([arXiv 2510.26658](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26658v1)). Kimi's "serial collapse" is the same failure. Left alone, models default to doing everything themselves.

How a running lead takes in results is where the evidence turns cautious. **Async-RBench** (arXiv 2610.32429, October 2026) ran nine agents on 200 tasks. Every one scored lower when subagent results arrived individually while it kept working than when all results arrived together: the mean fell from **29.7% to 26.0%**, and Claude Sonnet 5 went from 42.3 to 38.2. The weakest scenarios were duplicated or replayed reports (mean 8.6) and subagent failure (12.2). Prompting a full re-plan on each arrival recovered 2.6–3.5 points at 7.5–10.2% more model calls ([arXiv 2610.32429](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.32429v1)). **This source is unverified.** The note-taker read it, but its arXiv ID and model names could not be cross-checked independently, and the authors themselves say they do not show that async delivery *caused* the failures.

Evidence on interruptions points the same way. When a human's update arrives late in a reasoning model's thinking, performance falls by **up to 60%** ([arXiv 2510.11713](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11713), ICML 2026). On long web tasks, Opus 4.5 integrates real goal changes well, even late ones, while Haiku 4.5 drops from about 44% to about 24% success when the interruption comes at 80% of the way through instead of 20%, because smaller models "get entangled with the earlier, now-stale state" ([arXiv 2604.00892](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00892v1)). Steering helps when it carries information the agent needs. Unsolicited arrivals mid-reasoning cost something. No study measures a lead steering a subagent, as opposed to a human steering an agent.

The failure modes of async delegation are mostly classic distributed-systems ones. They include stale results used as final, duplicated reports, unhandled subagent failures, a lead that ends before integrating results or starts doing the work itself, orphaned workers after a restart, and lock contention on shared state. All appear in Claude Code's own list of known issues ([agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)) and in Cursor's write-ups. The mitigations that work live in code: deduplicate by worker, report each result once, cancel children when the lead stops, refuse delivery to a reused name, and send one structured handoff. Prompting the lead to be careful is not one of them. A related latency result, a 1.4× speedup from asynchronous function calls without model changes, comes from AsyncFC ([arXiv 2605.15077](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15077)), whose existence and numbers this report also could not cross-check.

## Peer messaging has no measured win and widens the attack surface

On topology, the controlled numbers favour a single verifying hub. Trace-level error amplification relative to a single agent is **4.4×** for centralized orchestration, **5.1×** for hybrid, **7.8×** for all-to-all peer debate and **17.2×** for independent agents with naive aggregation ([arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3)). Peer messaging clearly helped on only one benchmark: high-entropy parallel web search (BrowseComp-Plus, +9.2%). Success plateaued at about 0.39 messages per turn, and the authors call that pattern descriptive. The 2025 re-evaluations of debate agree that "majority voting alone accounts for most of the performance gains" credited to debate, which on its own does not raise expected correctness ([arXiv 2508.17536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17536)). Debate also often fails to beat chain-of-thought or self-consistency at higher compute ([arXiv 2502.08788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)). Neither abstract names its models, so treat both as mixed-era.

Cursor gives the clearest first-hand account. Twenty equal peers coordinating through a shared file with locks ran at "the effective throughput of two or three". With optimistic concurrency and no hierarchy, "agents became risk-averse", and nobody owned the hard problems. What worked was planners that can spawn sub-planners, workers that "don't coordinate with other workers", and a judge at each cycle boundary. An integrator role "created more bottlenecks than it solved" ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Every reversal found in the notes runs from flat to hierarchical, and none the other way. Claude Code ships peer mailboxes only as experimental agent teams. It recommends them for research, review and competing hypotheses, not for sequential work or edits to the same file, and it forbids nested teams ([docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)). There is no controlled evidence that depth beyond one helps. Cursor's recursive sub-planners are the only production example, and they deepen planning, not execution.

A shared taskboard works when one party writes and sizes the tasks and the board only arbitrates claims. Claude Code's lead-written task list still shows "task status lags", with teammates failing to mark work done. A self-organizing board without an owner failed at Cursor. Two 2025 blackboard papers report gains of 4–57% over static or master-slave baselines ([arXiv 2507.01701](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01701v1), [arXiv 2510.01285](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01285v2)), but both still route through a control unit, and the models behind them are not identified.

Security points the same way, on thinner evidence. The 2025–2026 papers model prompt injection as propagation over the communication graph, so every peer edge is another indirect-injection path ([arXiv 2504.00218](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.00218v2)). Topology-aware detection cuts attack success by **19.5–39.2%** ([arXiv 2502.11127](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11127v1)). Both figures come from abstracts and snippets, and no frontier-model study measures how injection spreads under a hierarchy against a mesh. The production control is clear, though. Claude Code marks a message from another agent as coming from another session, never lets it carry an approval, and has a classifier review messages between agents in auto mode ([docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)). A single 2026 preprint finds that no model managing a pool of subagents exceeds **50%** precision on workspace permissions ([arXiv 2606.31174](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31174v1)), which is an argument for keeping grant decisions away from the lead model.

## What Silo should keep, change and add

Silo's design already matches the converged production shape. Its lead prompt also echoes Anthropic's effort scaling ("Comparing or checking a few things takes 2–4 subagents"), Anthropic's results-by-reference advice (subagents "Save outputs to files … point to the files"), and the advice to verify rather than trust a summary. The code already handles several of the async failure modes in code rather than prompts. Reports are marked once (`reported`). The wake is debounced. Stops the lead initiated are quiet. A result is stored only while the row is still running. Names are unique per lead chat. A failed subagent's error text reaches the report (`runResult` falls back to the error event). The report is framed "This is not a message from the human".

| Depth 1 | Keep | No evidence that depth beyond 1 helps; Claude Code forbids nesting; Anthropic and Kimi run depth 1 | Moderate: absence of evidence |
| At most 8 live, with 2–4 the stated norm | Keep | Anthropic 3–5 in parallel; Claude Code 3–5 teammates; xAI 4 by default; Kimi's spurious-parallelism penalty | Moderate |
| Fresh context, trimmed tools, short report, outputs in files | Keep | Context isolation is the most consistently measured benefit (1–2K summaries; 67% fewer tokens) | Strong |
| Lead ends its turn and is woken; no `sleep` loop | Keep | Claude Code's background default; Cursor's unused wait tool; Cursor's handoff as a follow-up message | Strong as production practice; no controlled study |
| Running lead gets results at turn boundaries, not mid-turn | Keep | Late interruptions cost reasoning models up to 60%; unsolicited arrivals cost about 3.7 points in Async-RBench (unverified) | Moderate |
| Lead-written taskboard with `[NAME]` assignees | Keep | Boards work when one owner writes them; Cursor's ownerless board failed | Moderate |
| Approvals go to the human, never the lead | Keep | Claude Code bars agents from approving; managers under 50% permission precision | Moderate |
| `message_agent` steering | Keep, use sparingly | Anthropic named lack of steering as a cost; human-to-agent studies help only for real goal changes; no lead-to-subagent study | Thin |
| LEAD.md lists "build several files" as a case for subagents | Change | Every multi-agent topology lost on SWE-bench Verified (−2.1% to −14.9%); Cognition's single-writer rule; Anthropic rules out "most coding tasks" | Strong |
| Wake debounce of 750 ms batches only near-simultaneous finishes | Change | Async-RBench batched > per-result (unverified); each wake is a full lead turn, uncached after the provider's cache TTL | Moderate on cost; thin on quality |
| No independent-reviewer pattern | Add | Clean-context reviewer: 2 bugs per PR, 58% severe; a central verifier contains errors | Moderate (vendor-reported) |
| Report omits unfinished board items and filter verdicts | Add | Board status lag; injection crosses handoffs | Thin; reasoning from mechanism |

Four changes follow from that table. **First, narrow what LEAD.md invites.** Keep subagents for research, checking, exploration and review, plus building artifacts that do not depend on each other (separate reports or separate datasets). Keep changes inside one codebase or one shared document single-threaded: the lead, or one subagent, writes them. The current rule, "no two agents write the same files", prevents overwrites but not the conflicting implicit decisions Cognition describes. The prompt should also say outright what the saturation result implies: delegate for breadth, context isolation, wall-clock time or an independent check, never to make a strong model "smarter" on a task it can do alone.

**Second, batch wake-ups by spawn round.** Today a subagent that finishes three minutes after its sibling triggers a second full lead turn. If that comes after the provider's cache TTL, the lead re-reads its whole history uncached, which works against Silo's goal of conserving tokens. A better default is to wake the lead once every subagent from the same `spawn_agent` round has finished. Wake early on a failure, because failure handling scored worst in Async-RBench, and wake on a ceiling such as a few minutes. That ceiling answers the strongest objection, Cursor's design that was "bottlenecked by the slowest worker". The human still sees progress through the Bot's activity line, and a lead with its own share of the work still inspects results at its own turn boundaries.

**Third, document a reviewer pattern in LEAD.md.** It costs no new tool. After substantial writing, spawn one subagent with only the spec and the output paths, not the lead's reasoning, to check them. Give it a stronger model when the work matters.

**Fourth, harden the handoff in code.** For each finished agent, list in `buildReport` its `[NAME]` items left open on the board, read from the board rather than trusted from the agent's own account. If the subagent's run had results the output filter flagged or withheld, say so on its report line. A summary written from injected content otherwise reaches the lead as clean text. Extend SUBAGENT.md's final-report request to "deviations and discoveries that affect other agents' tasks", the field Cursor's handoffs carry.

Two things should not be added. A peer channel between subagents has no measured benefit. It raises cost, and it opens new paths for injection. If Silo ever wants competing hypotheses, run independent subagents and let the lead compare them: that is voting, which the debate literature credits with most of debate's gains. Grok 4.20 Multi-Agent is also not a fit for Silo's model allowlist as a chat or subagent model. It rejects client-side function calling, and every Silo tool is client-side, so it could serve at most as a tool-less consultant.

## Several cited sources need an asterisk

Some of the evidence above rests on older models or on sources the notes could not verify, and the recommendations lean on them unevenly. **MAST** (Cemri et al., 1,600+ failure traces, failure rates of 41–86.7%) uses GPT-4o-era and Claude 3.7 traces ([arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)). Its taxonomy is useful, but its rates should not be read as frontier behaviour, and no 2026 re-run exists. **AsyncLM** (December 2024, GPT-4o) and the resilience study that found hierarchies degrade least under faulty agents (GPT-3.5 era, [arXiv 2408.00989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.00989)) are historical. The debate re-evaluations, the blackboard papers and the "single agent with skills" paper ([arXiv 2601.04748](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04748v2), on saturated benchmarks such as GSM8K) do not name their models in what the notes read. **Async-RBench** and **AsyncFC** could not be cross-checked. The security figures come from abstracts or search snippets, and an aggregator's "Cohen's d = 2.34" result for topologies under injection is left out of this report because its original paper was never located. Vendor numbers (Anthropic's +90.2%, Kimi's swarm gains, Devin Review's bug counts, Cursor's scale figures) are self-reported on unpublished or in-house evals.

What is solid is the direction: Kim et al.'s frontier-model, token-matched study, the Nature Machine Intelligence extension to coding benchmarks, and the agreement of independent production teams on the hierarchical shape. What is missing is any frontier-era controlled comparison of peer messaging against hub-and-spoke at equal budgets, any ablation of depth, any study of a lead steering a subagent, and any measured cost of polling against notification.

## Conclusion

The field's question has shifted from "should agents collaborate?" to "where should the extra tokens go?" Parallel sampling with a judge (Grok Heavy, GPT-5 Pro, Deep Think) buys accuracy on hard single problems. Hub-and-spoke delegation buys breadth, wall-clock time and a protected lead context. Peer conversation buys nothing anyone has measured. For a token-conserving product, this means the question for each delegation is whether it shortens the critical path or protects the lead's context, which is Kimi's critical-steps idea used as a design rule. Saturation also means delegation matters less as frontier models improve, except where context limits bind or an independent second view is wanted.

Silo's architecture already sits where the evidence converges. Its remaining risks are not in topology. They lie in what the lead chooses to delegate (parallel writers) and in what each wake costs (per-result turns that miss the cache). Both are fixed with prompt wording and a change to the wake policy, not with new protocols. The open opportunity is the cheapest well-supported second agent: a reviewer with a clean context.
